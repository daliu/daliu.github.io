HUMANS ARE THE TRAINING DATA

Watch the machine learn.

AI and machine learning help software recognize patterns, make predictions and generate things. Kids, teens and adults all deserve a way to understand how that learning works—and where it can go wrong.

A system can look clumsy while it has little useful experience. With better examples and feedback, its behavior can improve until things seem to “click.” That is an analogy to human learning, not a claim that a machine thinks or understands like a person.

  1. ObserveYour weapons, movement affinities and clear times.
  2. CompareWhich tactics worked quickly? Which failed?
  3. AdaptTry counters and combine learned habits.
  4. Test againYou change tactics. So does the factory.

The mirror fights turn your habits into a new challenge: circuit crossfire, pursuit strikes, rolls, air-dash critical attempts and predictive guards. Fast, effective play gives the factory stronger evidence. These motor skills are authored; the local model learns affinities for movement families and changes their weights using your results. It does not memorize and replay an exact sequence of your buttons. During boss fights, a short observation window also calls support teams against sustained crouching, airtime or camping.

What actually runs here? A small statistical opponent model in your browser. It remembers a bounded history and adapts between encounters. It does not train a neural network, call an LLM or learn from other players. Optional gameplay statistics sharing is separate from this model; control it in Menu → Statistics. Many real AI products also do not keep learning during ordinary use.

Patterns can mislead. A tactic that worked once may fail against a new opponent. The point is to experiment, question the model, and notice the difference between copying a pattern and understanding it.
